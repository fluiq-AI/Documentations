We ran eight guardrails across four corpora and 949 cases. We came second on both output-side corpora and third on both input-side ones.

We are publishing all of it anyway: the harness, the corpora, the adapters, and every individual case each product missed, listed by ID. The full report is available as a PDF.

This post is the reasoning behind that decision, because the reasoning turns out to be more useful than the numbers.

The word for this is forty years old

Jim Gray coined benchmarketing in 1985 to describe using benchmarks as marketing weapons rather than engineering tools. Four decades later, the AI tooling market has rediscovered the practice with real enthusiasm.

DeepSource put a survey behind it in February 2026, in a piece bluntly titled Every AI code review vendor benchmarks itself, and wins. Their framing is the one I keep returning to:

Pharmaceutical companies don't design and run their own clinical trials. Self-evaluation is biased, even in good faith.

Note the phrase "even in good faith." Nobody in this pattern is lying. Bias enters through a hundred small, defensible choices. Which corpus. Which threshold. Which failure counts as out of scope. Which configuration of your own product you decide is the fair one to test. Each choice is individually reasonable, and their sum reliably points one direction.

The Greptile and Augment case makes it concrete: same repositories, different evaluation rules, opposite rankings. Both vendors published in good faith. Both won.

Endor Labs supplied the reframe that I think ends the argument:

"Did they win?" was never the question. The question is whether they give you enough to work out if the tool will solve your problem.

That is a testable property of a benchmark, unlike "is it honest."

What a self-published benchmark has to contain to be worth reading

Between DeepSource and Endor Labs there is a workable checklist. Six items:

1. The full corpora, downloadable.
2. Reproducible methodology, including the adapters.
3. Real ground truth: CVEs, bug-fix commits, human-validated cases, rather than synthetic data the vendor generated.
4. A vendor willing to say what their tool cannot do.
5. Controls and baselines.
6. Individual failures listed, not just aggregates.

Read that as a filter and most vendor benchmarks fail at item one. You cannot audit what you cannot download. Aggregate percentages with no corpus behind them are not a result. They are a claim wearing a result's clothes.

So we built ours to satisfy all six, and then had to live with what came out.

The setup

Eight guardrails. Four corpora. 949 cases. Every guardrail receives the same string and answers one question: block, or allow. Scoring was fixed before any contestant ran.

Two of the four corpora are public datasets nobody here curated: deepset/prompt-injections and jackhhao/jailbreak-classification, both Apache-2.0, plus a sample from ai4privacy/pii-masking-200k. We cannot have tuned to data we did not build, and you can check that claim yourself rather than taking it on trust.

Roughly a third of each output corpus is benign bait: order numbers shaped like card numbers, git SHAs shaped like secrets, sentences that merely describe an injection attempt without being one. This is deliberate, and it targets the failure mode the guardrail literature keeps flagging, which is over-defense. The InjecGuard work and its NotInject benchmark exist because a scanner that blocks everything scores beautifully on recall and is unusable in production. A benchmark containing only attacks measures paranoia and calls it security.

Second, second, third, third. On prompt injection specifically, a well-known commercial product detects nearly three times what we do.

If you are evaluating guardrails for input-side injection detection right now, and that is your only requirement, the table above tells you to look at Lakera rather than at us. I would rather you learn that here than three weeks into a trial.

The control is the most uncomfortable row on the page

We included a 30-line regex baseline because item five on the checklist says to include controls, and because a benchmark with no floor cannot tell you whether anything is actually working.

On the adversarial output corpus it scores 62.5% recall at 11.8% false alarms, beating three of the eight contestants, one of which is a commercial product.

Thirty lines of regular expressions. No model, no API call, no per-seat pricing.

That is a finding we would have every commercial incentive to leave out, and it is the single most useful row in the table for a reader deciding whether to buy anything at all. On the input-side corpora it collapses to roughly zero, which is the honest counterpoint. Regex is a real floor for structured leakage and no floor whatsoever for semantic attacks. Knowing which of those problems you actually have determines whether you need to spend money.

Four methodology decisions, and what each one cost us

We rank the configuration that ships, not our best one. We measured more than one configuration of our own gate. The one that competes in the tables is the one that runs when you call fluiq.secure(), because nobody runs Lakera twice with its model switched off and reports the better half. The other configurations score lower and are published rather than quietly dropped. Our inline scanner reaches 68.8% recall at 5.9% false alarms on the adversarial suite and 39.0% at 0.0% on the public PII corpus. The API fast path reaches 13.3% on injection and 42.0% on jailbreak. Those numbers are worse. They are in the report.

Nightfall's first run was thrown away. It errored on 190 of 300 cases through rate limiting. We could have published that as a poor score, and it would have looked like a competitor performing badly. Instead we discarded the run, added backoff, and re-ran. The harness now refuses to rank any contestant that errors on more than 2% of a corpus. A rate limit is not a detection failure, and scoring it as one is how you accidentally publish a lie.

Lakera's jailbreak numbers get an explanation rather than a victory lap. 97.3% recall at 83.3% false alarms looks catastrophic until you read the corpus. The benign half contains a large number of persona and roleplay prompts, along the lines of "you are Black Panther," which this benchmark labels benign and Lakera treats as hostile. That single difference of opinion produces both the high recall and the high false-alarm rate.

That is a labelling disagreement, not a defect. If your product is a children's education app, Lakera's position may well be the correct one and this benchmark's labels are wrong for you. We are not going to pretend a disagreement about policy is a competitor's bug, because the moment we do, every number on the page becomes a marketing artifact.

Scope differences are stated, not scored. Presidio detects PII and never claimed to detect API keys, so it scores zero on secrets. That is a scope difference. It is why per-corpus results exist and why no single aggregate number should be read alone, including ours.

What this costs, and why we think it is worth it

The obvious objection: publishing a benchmark you do not win is a strange way to sell a product.

Three reasons it is the right call.

This audience is unusually good at detecting the alternative. Senior engineers and CTOs have seen the vendor-benchmark genre. A page where the publisher wins every category does not read as strong. It reads as a page where you cannot tell what the tool actually does. Every number becomes suspect, including the true ones.

Every capability claim is more credible sitting next to a stated limitation. Our output-side gate is genuinely competitive, and you are more likely to believe that after reading our injection row than instead of it. That is not a rhetorical trick. It is how trust works when the reader can check.

It is the same discipline we ask of our users. We tell people to calibrate their judges against human labels, to include baselines, to route failures back into the test set, and to distrust a metric they have not audited. It would be difficult to hold that position while publishing a benchmark we designed to win.

Three questions to ask any benchmark, ours included

Can I download the corpus? If not, stop reading. Everything downstream is unverifiable.

Is there a control? Without a floor you cannot tell whether the winner is good or the field is weak. If the naive baseline beats half the products, that is the most actionable thing on the page.

Does the publisher say anything unflattering about themselves? Not hedging language. A specific number where they lose. If a benchmark contains no such number, the absence is the result.

Guardrails change, so every number here is dated. Measured 7 August 2026. When we re-run it, the numbers get restated, including the ones that move against us.

"Did they win?" was never the question.
